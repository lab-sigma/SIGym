Trading Game Analysis

This interactive environment simulates a market making game. Your objective as the investor is to maximize your profit against a market maker with the same goal. Every round, the market maker puts out a bid (price they buy at) and an ask (price they sell at). You must then respond by buying or selling a number of contracts to them.

At the start of the game, a random set of cards is drawn from the deck. The true market price is represented by the sum of these cards, and is unknown until the very end of the game. Each round, one additional card will be revealed.

In order to maximize their chances of winning, the investor wants to:

\(\quad\) (a) Sell at a price higher than the true market price.
\(\quad\) (b) Buy at a price lower than true price.
\(\quad\) (c) Hold when the true price is within the bid ask spread.

To do this, they must predict the true price (determined by the sum of all drawn cards) using the information available each round.

Calculating expected value of true price

We can predict the value of the true price by averaging the sum over all remaining cards, multiplying by the number of hidden cards, then adding this value to the sum of the cards already revealed.

Let...
\(n\): Current round number
\(k\): Sum of all cards in deck
\(N\): Number of all cards in deck
\(C_i\): Value of \(i_{th}\) revealed card
\(r\): Number of rounds (total cards pulled from deck)

The formula for the expected value of the true price \(X\) given \(n\) revealed cards is as follows:

$$\mathbb{E}[X|R_n] = \frac{k - \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i}{N-n} \cdot (r-n) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i $$

Basic Strategy

Let...
\(M_n\): Expected price calculated by market maker for round \(n\)
\(I_n\): Expected price calculated by investor for round \(n\)
\(u_p\): Utility for player \(p\)
\(R_n\): Set of cards revealed up to round \(n\)
\(X\): True price

Given a bid-ask spread \(d\), the bid price \(b\) and ask price \(a\) determined by the market maker are defined as follows.
$$b = M_n - d$$ $$a = M_n + d$$ For simplicity, assume \(d = 0\), so \(b = a = M_n\).

Then the investor's strategy can be written as:

If \(I_n > M_n\): then buy $$u_i = \mathbb{E}[I_n - M_n]$$ If \(I_n < M_n\): then sell $$u_i = \mathbb{E}[M_n - I_n]$$

The expected profit (or utility) of the investor is then as follows:

$$u_i = \mathbb{E}[I_n-M_n] \cdot P(I_n > M_n) + \mathbb{E}[M_n-I_n] \cdot P(I_n < M_n)$$

Let \(q = P(I_n > M_n)\)
Simplified,
\begin{equation} \label{eq1} \begin{split} u_i & = \mathbb{E}[I_n-M_n] \cdot q + \mathbb{E}[M_n-I_n] \cdot (1 - q) \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_nq - M_nq] + \mathbb{E}[M_n(1 - q) - I_n(1 - q)] \\ & = \mathbb{E}[2I_nq - 2M_nq + M_n - I_n] \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_n - M_n](2q-1) \end{split} \end{equation}

Fair game

For a fair game where the market maker and investor are exposed to the same information, both players will utilize the calculation resulting in the exact same expected true price. Therefore the investor will never trade, and the utility (expected profit) of both players will be 0.

For \(I_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_n] = M_n\): \begin{equation} \begin{split} u_i & = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|R_n] - \mathbb{E}[X|R_n]](2q-1) \\ & = \mathbb{E}[0](2q-1) \\ & = 0 \\ \end{split} \end{equation}
For \(I_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_n] = M_n\): \begin{equation} \begin{split} u_i & = \mathbb{E}[I_n - a] \cdot P(I_n > a) + \mathbb{E}[b - I_n] \cdot P(I_n < b) \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_n - (I_n + d)] \cdot P(I_n > I_n + d) + \mathbb{E}[I_n - d - I_n] \cdot P(I_n < I_n - d) \\ & = \mathbb{E}[-d] \cdot 0 + \mathbb{E}[-d] \cdot 0 \\ & = 0 \\ \\ u_m & = d \cdot P(I_n > I_n + d) + d \cdot P(I_n < I_n - d) \\ & = d \cdot 0 + d \cdot 0 \\ & = 0 \end{split} \end{equation}

Introducing an Information Advantage

To make the game more interesting, we can give the market maker an information advantage by allowing them to see 1 card ahead of the investor.

Bid (b) = \(M_n - d\)
Ask (a) = \(M_n + d\)

The investor's strategy can be written as:

If \(I_n > a\): then buy
$$u_i = \mathbb{E}[I_n - a]$$ $$u_m = \mathbb{E}[a - M_n] = d$$ If \(I_n < b\): then sell
$$u_i = \mathbb{E}[b - I_n]$$ $$u_m = \mathbb{E}[M_n - b] = d$$

The expected utilities of the investor and market maker are then as follows:

$$u_i = \mathbb{E}[I_n - a] \cdot P(I_n > a) + \mathbb{E}[b - I_n] \cdot P(I_n < b)$$ $$u_m = d \cdot P(I_n > a) + d \cdot P(I_n < b)$$

Let \(q_a = P(I_n > a)\), \(q_b = P(I_n < b)\)
\begin{equation} \begin{split} u_i & = \mathbb{E}[I_n - a] \cdot q_a + \mathbb{E}[b - I_n] \cdot q_b \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_n q_a - a q_a] + \mathbb{E}[b q_b - I_n q_b] \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_n q_a - q_a(M_n + d) + q_b(M_n - d) - I_n q_b] \\ & = \mathbb{E}[I_n q_a - I_n q_b - M_n q_a + M_n q_b - d q_a - d q_b] \\ & = (q_a - q_b) \cdot \mathbb{E}[I_n - M_n] - d(q_a + q_b) \\ \end{split} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \begin{split} u_m & = d \cdot q_a + d \cdot q_b \\ & = d(q_a+q_b) \\ \end{split} \end{equation}

Naive Investor vs Advantaged MM

Naive investor's strategy:

For \(I_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_n]\), \(M_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]\):
\begin{equation} \begin{split} u_m & = d(q_a+q_b) \\ & = d(P(I_n > M_n + d) + P(I_n < M_n - d)) \\ & = d(P(\mathbb{E}[X|R_n] > \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] + d) + P(\mathbb{E}[X|R_n] < \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] - d)) \\ \end{split} \end{equation}

\begin{multline} P(I_n > M_n + d) = P(\frac{k - \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i}{N-n} \cdot (r-n) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i \\ > \frac{k - \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} C_i}{N-(n+1)} \cdot (r-(n+1)) + \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} C_i) \\ \end{multline}

In this scenario, the market maker can optimize bid-ask spread to maximize their utility.

Optimal Investor vs Advantaged MM

Optimal investor's strategy:

For \(I_n = M_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]\):
\begin{equation} \begin{split} u_m & = d(q_a+q_b) \\ & = d(P(I_n > M_n + d) + P(I_n < M_n - d)) \\ & = d(P(\mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] > \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] + d) + P(\mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] < \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] - d)) \\ & = d(0+0) \\ & = 0 \\ \\ u_i & = (q_a - q_b) \cdot \mathbb{E}[I_n - M_n] - d(q_a + q_b) \\ & = (P(I_n > M_n + d) - P(I_n < M_n - d)) \cdot \mathbb{E}[I_n - M_n] - 0 \\ & = 0 \cdot \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}] - \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]] \\ & = 0 \\ \end{split} \end{equation}

The information advantage would not be a true advantage against an optimal investor, as they would be able to derive the information from the market maker's move. Such an investor would continue to hold every round, as it is clear that taking any action would be a disadvantage.

Given a perfectly optimal investor, the utility of both the market maker and investor will still be 0. No player truly has an advantage in this scenario. Due to the zero-sum nature of the game, this solution is the trivial Nash Equilibrium.

Assuming \(d=0\):

For \(I_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_n]\), \(M_n = \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]\): \begin{equation} \begin{split} u_i & = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|R_n] - \mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]](2q-1) \\ & = (\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|R_n]] - \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X|R_{n+1}]])(2q-1) \\ & = (\mathbb{E}[X] - \mathbb{E}[X])(2q-1) \\ & = 0 \\ \end{split} \end{equation}
